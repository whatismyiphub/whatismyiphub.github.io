What Is an ISP and How Does It Shape Your Internet?

ISP stands for Internet Service Provider — the company that connects your home or phone to the internet. When you pay a monthly bill for broadband or mobile data, you are paying an ISP. It owns or leases the cables, towers, and equipment that carry your traffic, it gives you your IP address, and it is the first step on every journey your data takes across the internet. Understanding what your ISP does explains a surprising amount about how your connection behaves.

Your ISP's most visible job is speed. The plan you buy — 10 Mbps, 50 Mbps, 100 Mbps — is the maximum speed the ISP will deliver to your home. But speed is not the only thing that matters. Latency, the delay before data starts moving, decides how responsive video calls and games feel. Reliability decides whether your connection drops at the worst moment. And the quality of the ISP's connection to the rest of the internet decides how fast websites in other countries load, even when your local speed is excellent.

Your ISP also assigns your IP address, and that assignment carries information. The address reveals your approximate location and the name of your ISP to every website you visit, which is why our homepage tool can show both. It also means your ISP knows a great deal about you: which sites you visit, when you are online, and how much data you use. In most countries, ISPs are legally required to keep some of these records, and they can be asked to share them with authorities. This is one of the main reasons people use a VPN — to keep their browsing private from their own provider.

Not all ISPs are equal. A fibre provider that runs its own cables to your street can offer faster, more reliable service than one that resells an old copper network. A mobile provider with a strong signal in your area will beat one with a weak one, whatever the advertised speeds say. Before choosing, ask neighbours what they use, check independent speed-test results for your area, and read the fine print: some plans slow down after a data limit, some charge extra for a static IP address, and some lock you into a long contract.

Your ISP also controls a few things you might not expect. It runs the DNS servers that turn website names into addresses, unless you change them to a public alternative. It can block websites that a court or regulator orders it to block. And in some countries it is allowed to prioritise some kinds of traffic over others, though net neutrality rules in many places forbid this. If a website loads slowly on one provider and fast on another, the difference is often in these invisible choices.

Choosing an ISP is one of the most consequential technology decisions in a household, and it is worth more than a glance at the advertised price. Look at real speeds in your area, ask about reliability and support, check whether the plan suits how you actually use the internet, and remember that the cheapest option is rarely the best value. Your ISP is the foundation of your online life — everything else you do on the internet is built on top of it.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does an ISP do?

It connects you to the internet, assigns your IP address, and carries your traffic to and from the rest of the world.

Can my ISP see what I do online?

Yes, it can see which sites you visit and how much data you use. A VPN hides the details from your ISP.

Why is my internet slow if my plan is fast?

The plan is a maximum. Congestion, Wi-Fi problems, the website's own servers, and your ISP's links to the wider internet can all slow things down.
