A Short History of IP Addresses: From ARPANET to Today

The IP address is one of the oldest ideas on the internet, and its history is the history of the internet itself. It began in the 1970s, when researchers building ARPANET — the military research network that became the internet — needed a way for computers to find each other. The solution, published in 1981, was the Internet Protocol: every computer would have a unique 32-bit number, written as four decimal numbers separated by dots. The first addresses were handed out by hand, recorded in a notebook, to a network of a few hundred machines.

For the first decade, addresses were generous. The system divided them into classes: large organisations got millions of addresses, medium ones got thousands, and small ones got hundreds. Universities, governments, and early technology companies received enormous blocks that they still hold today. Nobody worried about running out, because the internet was a research project, and four billion addresses seemed infinite.

The web changed everything. When the World Wide Web arrived in the early 1990s, the internet began doubling in size every year. The class system was replaced by a more efficient method called CIDR, which let addresses be divided into blocks of any size. Network Address Translation arrived, letting whole homes and offices share a single address. And still the addresses ran out: the central pool was exhausted in 2011, and the regional registries followed over the next few years.

The response was IPv6, designed in the 1990s and standardised in 1998. It expanded addresses from 32 bits to 128, giving the internet more addresses than it could ever use, and it simplified several parts of the protocol along the way. But adoption was slow, because IPv6 and IPv4 are not compatible, and the whole world had to upgrade while staying connected. Today, IPv6 carries a large and growing share of traffic, and the transition continues — a decades-long project that is still under way.

Along the way, the humble IP address picked up jobs its designers never imagined. It became the basis for geolocation, for fraud detection, for content licensing, and for advertising. It became a piece of personal data, protected by privacy laws in Europe and elsewhere. And it became a subject of public debate, in arguments about surveillance, censorship, and the right to be anonymous online.

The history of the IP address is a reminder that the internet was built by people solving the problem in front of them, not by people who could see the future. A 32-bit number designed for a few hundred research computers ended up connecting billions of people — and when it ran out, the internet adapted, as it always has. The address in your browser today is the descendant of a notebook entry from 1981, and it still does the same job: telling the internet where to find you.

Frequently Asked Questions

When was the IP address invented?

The Internet Protocol was published in 1981, for the ARPANET research network that became the internet.

Why are there two versions, IPv4 and IPv6?

IPv4 ran out of addresses. IPv6 was designed with effectively unlimited addresses to replace it, and the two now run side by side.

Who hands out IP addresses?

Five regional registries allocate blocks to internet providers, who assign them to customers. The system is coordinated globally.
