What Is My IP Address? A Plain-English Explanation

An IP address is your device's address on the internet. Just as a letter needs a postal address to reach your house, data travelling across the internet needs an address to reach your computer, phone, or tablet. That address is a set of numbers — for example, 103.86.12.45 — and it is how websites, apps, and other computers know where to send the information you asked for. Without it, the internet could not work at all.

IP stands for Internet Protocol, the set of rules that governs how data moves online. Every device connected to the internet has an IP address, though what you see when you check yours is usually the public address of your home router or mobile network, shared by all the devices behind it. Inside your home, each device also has a private address, like 192.168.1.5, which only works on your local network. The public address is the one the outside world sees.

Your public IP address reveals two things: roughly where you are, and who provides your internet. The location comes from a database that maps address ranges to cities and countries, and it is usually accurate to the city level — sometimes to the neighbourhood, rarely to the street. The provider, or ISP, is the company that owns the address range, such as PTCL, Jazz, or Zong in Pakistan. Our homepage tool shows both, using the public services ipify and ipapi.co, and it says plainly that the location is approximate.

IP addresses come in two versions. IPv4, the original, uses four numbers separated by dots, and it has room for about 4.3 billion addresses — a number that seemed enormous in the 1980s and ran out years ago. IPv6, the successor, uses eight groups of hexadecimal characters separated by colons, and it has room for so many addresses that we will never run out. Most of the internet still runs on IPv4, but IPv6 adoption grows every year, and many networks now give you both.

Your IP address is not fixed forever. Most home connections use dynamic addresses, which means your provider can change your address from time to time — when your router restarts, for example. Businesses and servers often pay for a static address that never changes, because other people need to find them reliably. Neither is better in itself; they serve different purposes, and knowing which kind you have helps you understand what your address can and cannot do.

Finally, it is worth knowing that your IP address is visible to every website you visit. That is normal — it is how the internet works — but it is also why people talk about IP privacy. A website can log your address, and an advertiser can use it to guess your city. If that bothers you, a VPN can hide your real address behind another one, though it comes with its own trade-offs. For most people, the practical step is simply to know what an IP address is, to check it occasionally, and to understand what it does and does not reveal about you.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I find my IP address?

Use the tool on our homepage. It fetches your public address and shows your approximate location and provider.

Is my IP address the same as my home address?

No. It reveals your approximate city and your internet provider, not your street address or your name.

Can my IP address change?

Yes, if your connection uses a dynamic address. Restarting your router often gives you a new one.
