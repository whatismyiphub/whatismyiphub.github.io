IPv4 Exhaustion: How the Internet Ran Out of Addresses

In 1981, when the internet's address system was designed, 4.3 billion addresses seemed like more than anyone could ever need. The internet was a small research network, and the idea that every person on Earth would carry a connected computer in their pocket was science fiction. By 2011, the last large blocks of IPv4 addresses had been handed out. The internet had run out — and the story of how that happened, and how we coped, is one of the great quiet dramas of technology.

The shortage was not a surprise. Engineers saw it coming in the 1990s, as the web exploded and mobile phones arrived. The first response was NAT, Network Address Translation, which lets hundreds of devices share a single public address. Your home router does this: every device in your house shares one public address, and the router sorts out which reply belongs to which device. NAT bought the internet decades of extra life, and it is still the reason IPv4 works today despite the shortage.

The second response was a market. Because addresses became scarce, they became valuable. Companies that held large blocks they no longer needed began selling them to companies that did, at prices that rose from a few dollars per address to fifty dollars or more. A block of addresses that was once given away free became an asset worth millions. This market kept the internet growing, but it also meant that new providers and new countries had to pay heavily for the addresses they needed.

The third response was IPv6, designed from the start with effectively unlimited addresses. But moving the whole internet to a new system while it kept running was like rebuilding an aeroplane in flight, and it has taken decades. The transition is still under way: IPv6 now carries a large share of traffic in many countries, but IPv4 remains essential, and the two systems will run side by side for years to come.

For ordinary users, the exhaustion is mostly invisible. Your provider manages it: you share an address through NAT, or you have both IPv4 and IPv6, and everything works. But there are signs if you look. Some mobile networks put you behind a stricter form of NAT, which can cause problems with gaming and video calls. Some new services are IPv6-only, and if your provider does not support it, you cannot reach them. And the price of IPv4 addresses is one reason some smaller providers charge extra for a static address.

The lesson is a humbling one for anyone who builds systems. A limit that looked absurdly generous — four billion of anything — turned out to be too small, because the world changed faster than the designers imagined. IPv6 was built with that lesson in mind: its address space is so large that running out is not a realistic concern for any future we can imagine. But the history of IPv4 is a reminder that in technology, forever is usually shorter than you think.

Frequently Asked Questions

When did IPv4 run out?

The central pool was exhausted in 2011, and the regional registries ran out over the following years. New allocations are now only available by transfer.

How does the internet still work with no IPv4 addresses left?

NAT lets many devices share one public address, and IPv6 provides new addresses. Together they keep the internet growing.

Will IPv4 ever be switched off?

Not in the foreseeable future. The two systems will run side by side for many years, because so much equipment still depends on IPv4.
