IPv6 Explained: Why the Internet Needed a New Address System

The internet ran out of addresses. That is the simple reason IPv6 exists. The original system, IPv4, uses 32-bit addresses — four numbers, each from 0 to 255 — which allows about 4.3 billion unique addresses. That sounded inexhaustible in 1981, when the internet was a research project with a few hundred computers. By 2011, the last blocks were being handed out, and the world had billions of phones, laptops, and smart devices all needing an address of their own.

IPv6 solves the shortage by using 128-bit addresses: eight groups of four hexadecimal digits, separated by colons, like 2001:0db8:85a3:0000:0000:8a2e:0370:7334. The number of possible addresses is 340 undecillion — so large that every grain of sand on Earth could have its own address, many times over. The length looks intimidating at first, but most of the time you never type an IPv6 address: your devices and your browser handle it silently, and addresses can be shortened by dropping leading zeroes and compressing runs of them.

The transition has been slow because IPv4 and IPv6 do not talk to each other directly. A device speaking only IPv4 cannot reach a server speaking only IPv6, and vice versa. So for years the internet has run both systems side by side, in a period called dual-stack: your connection has an IPv4 address and an IPv6 address, and your device picks whichever the destination supports. That is why the move has taken decades rather than happening overnight — everything had to keep working while it changed.

For ordinary users, IPv6 brings quiet benefits. There is no more need for NAT, the trick that lets many devices share one public IPv4 address: with IPv6, every device can have its own public address, which makes peer-to-peer connections, gaming, and video calls simpler and sometimes faster. IPv6 also has better support for mobile devices moving between networks, and its design includes security features that were bolted on later in IPv4. You will not notice most of this, which is exactly the point — it is infrastructure, not a feature.

Whether you have IPv6 depends on your provider and your router. Many providers in Europe and Asia now offer it by default, while others still do not. Our IPv4 vs IPv6 checker tool shows which protocol you are using right now, and whether your connection supports both. If you only have IPv4, everything will keep working for the foreseeable future — the internet is not about to switch IPv4 off. But if your provider offers IPv6, turning it on in your router settings is a sensible step towards the future.

The lesson of IPv6 is a familiar one in technology: a limit that looked impossibly far away arrived sooner than anyone expected, and the fix took years of patient, invisible work. The next time you see a long string of hexadecimal digits in a network setting, you will know what it is — the internet's answer to running out of room, built to last for generations.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between IPv4 and IPv6?

IPv4 uses 32-bit addresses (about 4.3 billion total). IPv6 uses 128-bit addresses (effectively unlimited) and is the long-term replacement.

Do I need IPv6?

Not urgently. IPv4 still works everywhere. But IPv6 is the future, and enabling it if your provider offers it is a good idea.

Why is an IPv6 address so long?

It is 128 bits written as eight groups of hexadecimal digits. The length is what gives it an effectively unlimited number of addresses.
