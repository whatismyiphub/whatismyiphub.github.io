How Accurate Is IP Location? What Your Address Really Reveals

Type your IP address into a lookup tool and it will tell you a city, a region, and sometimes a postcode. It feels precise, but it is not. IP location is an educated guess, built from databases that map address ranges to places, and its accuracy varies wildly. Understanding how it works — and where it goes wrong — helps you read those results with the right amount of trust.

The databases are built from several sources. Internet registries record which provider owns each block of addresses and where that provider registered them. Providers sometimes publish the cities where their equipment sits. And some databases use measurements: if a device with a known GPS location uses a certain address, the database learns that the address is near that place. Combining these signals gives a reasonable estimate, usually correct to the city level in urban areas.

But there are many ways for the guess to be wrong. A mobile network may route all its users in a country through equipment in one city, so every phone appears to be in the capital. A provider may register a block of addresses at its head office, hundreds of kilometres from where they are actually used. A corporate network may show the location of its headquarters rather than the branch you sit in. And if you use a VPN or a company network, the location shown is the server's, not yours at all.

The typical accuracy is this: country is almost always right, region or state is usually right, and city is right most of the time in large cities — perhaps seventy to ninety percent. Postcode or street-level claims should be treated with suspicion; IP location simply is not that precise. If a lookup tool claims to know your street from your IP address alone, it is either guessing from a very small area or it is wrong. Real precision needs GPS, which is a different system entirely.

This imprecision is also a kind of protection. Your IP address cannot reveal your home address, your name, or your exact location. It can reveal your city and your provider, which is enough for a website to show you local prices or the right language, but not enough to find your door. That is why IP location is used for broad decisions — which version of a site to show, which adverts are relevant — and never for anything that needs real precision, like navigation or emergency services.

The next time a website greets you with the wrong city, you will know why. Its database is out of date, or your provider routes your traffic through another city, or you are on a network that centralises its traffic. It is not a fault in your connection, and it is not a sign that someone is tracking you precisely. IP location is a rough map, not a sat-nav — useful for the broad picture, and honest about its limits.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can someone find my home address from my IP?

No. IP location is accurate to a city at best. Your street address is not connected to your IP address in any public database.

Why does an IP lookup show the wrong city?

Your provider may route traffic through equipment in another city, or the database may be out of date or based on the provider's registration address.

Is IP location the same as GPS?

No. GPS is precise to a few metres and needs your permission. IP location is a rough estimate made from databases, with no permission needed.
